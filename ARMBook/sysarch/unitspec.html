Systems Architecture

Level: 1
Credits: 20

Pre-requisite and Co-requisite Units

Pre-requisites

None

Co-requisites

Programming and Software Engineering.

Aims

To provide a general introduction to Computer Organisation, introducing logic gates, data representation, CPU structure and low level programming to provide an understanding of the underlying architecture of a computer system in order to support other units on the course.

Intended Learning Outcomes

At the end of the unit the learner is expected to be able to:
  1. Describe the format of simple data structures and their limitations.

  2. Describe the relationship between the process, bus, memory and input-output devices.

  3. Understand the relationship between computer hardware and software.

  4. Relate basic constructs of high-level programming language to their low level implementation.

  5. Explain how a computer system processes real-time events.

Learning and Teaching Methods

Intellectual, Cognitive and Operational Development

The context is a single-processor, single-user computer, typically an embedded micro controller. The unit concentrates on the Architecture component of the Computing benchmark.

The unit starts with a general introduction to low level computing, leading on to a detailed discussion on hardware starting with the basic logic gates leading though combinational and sequential logic up to a simple shift register. The basic data formats are then introduced, allowing the learner to understand how the hardware can be used to support data and the limitations this introduces.

A block level CPU is then introduced allowing the unit to progress on to a detailed discussion of the CPU architecture, leading to the introduction of low level programming as a method of controlling the system.

Finally programmed and automatic Input / Output is introduced, including the handling of real-time events.

Indicative Styles

Learners are presented with a series of examples in the formal course material, which is designed to allow the learner to progress through the course in small incremental steps.

Learners undertake a series of exercises, which are based on the formal examples and are designed to re-enforce the incremental steps demonstrated in the example. Learners are encouraged to keep a "day book" to note their experiments and answers to the exercises.

A CASE tool is provided for the learner to experiment with circuit design and to carry out the exercises. A cross assembler, simulator, and debugger package is provided for the learner to experiment with low level programming and to carry out the exercises.

Laboratory sessions are used to discuss model answers to selected exercises, and to provide informal feedback and support for the assessment.

Feedback on progress

Learners are encouraged to keep a lab book to note their experiments and answers to the exercises. Assistance is only given when learners can demonstrate though the use of the lab book their own attempts at resolving the problem. Learners are encouraged to compare the model answer to the exercises with their own and participate in the discussion of the model answer.

Assessment Methods

Assessment: Intended Learning Outcomes 1 though 5:

All of these Intended Learning Outcomes can effectively be assessed through written work. At this level the learner is expected to be able to demonstrate a broad understanding, and written assessment that requires them to explain or describe is appropriate.

Assessment: Intended Learning Outcomes 1 though 5:

Two practical exercises are structured to allow the learner to view the same application from the two different perspectives, hardware and software. The learner is expected to demonstrate in practical terms their understanding of the Intended Learning Outcomes.

Assessment: Weighting

The balance between coursework and examination is 30:70.

Indicative Content

Hardware:

Basic Logic Gates, Combinational Logic, the tri-state and the bus, Flip-Flops, Clocking, Sequential Logic, Examples of Sequential Logic, CPU Architecture, the fetch/execute cycle, microcode, CISC vs RISC, pipelining, cache, memory.

Data Representation:

Bits, bytes, and words. Binary, octal, hexadecimal and decimal. Integers, signed and unsigned, sign and magnitude, two's complement. Fractions vs Decimal numbers, fixed- and floating-point. Characters, both ASCII and UniCode.

Low Level Programming:

Assembly Language, Instructions vs Data vs Addresses, Psudocode, and Addressing Modes. Processor Instructions, Data Movement, Simple Arithmetic, Extended Arithmetic, Shift Operations, Logical/Binary Operations, Flow Control Privileged Instructions. The stack, subroutines, parameter passing, and interrupts. Input and Output methods, Direct Memory Access, Interrupt Driven, Polled.
